# Module 03 — Composition and Visual Continuity in Chinese Calligraphy (章法·結字·筆法)

**Scope.** This module documents how Chinese calligraphy organises visual material at three nested scales — the stroke (用筆), the character (結字 / 間架結構), and the whole composition (章法) — and then turns that knowledge into a practical method for matching a newly drawn character to existing calligraphy, for example when a family-history book about the Qi/Chee (齊) family needs a title, colophon or page heading lettered to sit convincingly beside an ancestor's handwriting.

**Claim labels used throughout:** (A) documented fact or primary statement, (B) visual observation, (C) scholarly interpretation, (D) proposed contemporary design application.

---

## 1. The three scales of a written composition

Chinese calligraphy theory treats a written work as a hierarchy in which each level constrains the next (A):

| Scale | Chinese term | What it governs | Classic codification |
|---|---|---|---|
| Stroke | 用筆 / 筆法 | How each line is begun, driven and finished | 《筆陣圖》 tradition; 永字八法 "Eight Principles of Yong" |
| Character | 結字 / 間架結構 | Balance, density, posture of one character | 智果《心成頌》(attrib.); 歐陽詢《三十六法》; 李淳《大字結構八十四法》; 邵瑛/黃自元《間架結構摘要九十二法》 |
| Composition | 章法 / 布白 | Columns, spacing, rhythm, seals, inscriptions | 鄧石如「計白當黑」; 孫過庭《書譜》 |

A Ming critic's dictum, quoted in a modern study of character structure, puts the relationship bluntly: "One who can construct but not handle the brush can still achieve a coherent form; one who only knows brushwork but not structure cannot form a character at all" (能結構不能用筆，猶得成體；若但知用筆，不知結構，全不成形矣) — Zhao Hanguang 趙宦光, *Hanshan Tiaotan* (A).[^1^] The same source compares a character's 間架 to the timber frame of a house: the site determines the dimensions of the beams (A).[^1^]

## 2. 結字 / 間架結構: the engineered balance of one character

### 2.1 The canonical manuals

- **《心成頌》**, attributed to the Sui monk Zhiguo 智果, and the **《三十六法》** (Thirty-Six Methods), traditionally attributed to Ouyang Xun 歐陽詢 (557–641), are the earliest systematic statements of character construction (A).[^2^] A Japanese calligraphy manual preserved on Wikimedia Commons notes that the Thirty-Six Methods is almost certainly a late-Song compilation riding on Ouyang Xun's authority — the text mentions Su Dongpo, who lived four centuries after Ouyang — but that its content faithfully codifies the structural logic of the Ouyang manner (C).[^3^] Designers should therefore treat the *methods* as a grammar, not as autograph instructions.
- **Li Chun 李淳**, Ming dynasty, expanded the scheme to **84 methods** (《大字結構八十四法》) (A).[^2^]
- The most widely circulated manual, **《間架結構摘要九十二法》** (Ninety-Two Methods), was long credited to the late-Qing calligrapher **Huang Ziyuan 黃自元 (1837–1918)**. Modern research based on the Qianlong-era carved stones in the Beijing Stone Carving Art Museum attributes the compilation to **Shao Ying 邵瑛 (1739–1818)**, dated 1784; Huang Ziyuan re-wrote and published it in 1884 (his copy is marked 錄舊本, "transcribed from an old edition"), which is why his name stuck (C).[^4^]

### 2.2 Representative rules and what they mean visually

The rules are not decorative recipes; each is a solution to a specific optical problem. A selection from the Thirty-Six Methods (wording per the Republican-era 《書法指南》 edition) and the Ninety-Two Methods:[^2^][^5^]

| Rule | Literal meaning | The optical problem it solves |
|---|---|---|
| 排疊 (36-fa #1) | Stack strokes evenly | In stroke-dense characters (壽, 畫, 麗, 爨) every horizontal must be spaced so no two merge and no gap dominates — "分間布白", apportion the white (A).[^2^] |
| 避就 (#2) | Avoid and yield | When a character repeats a stroke type (two left-fallings in 廬, two right-fallings in 頗), one must be shortened or redirected so they don't read as a stutter (A).[^5^] |
| 頂戴 (#3) | Carrying on the head | Top-heavy characters (疊, 藥) must stand steady — heavy above, light below, without toppling (A).[^2^] |
| 向背 (#5) | Facing vs. back-turned | Paired components either lean toward each other (好, 卯) or brace apart (北, 兆); the posture must be committed, not ambiguous (A).[^5^] |
| 相讓 (#8) | Mutual yielding | In left–right compounds the fuller side cedes space: the 口 in 嗚 sits high, in 和 it sits low, so neighbours never collide (A).[^5^] |
| 意連 (#15) | Idea connected though strokes are broken | Characters whose strokes never touch (之, 心, 小, 川, 水) are written so the *intended path of the brush* still links them — the eye completes the motion (A).[^5^] |
| 借換 (#18) / 增減 (#19) | Borrowing, swapping, adding, omitting | Strokes may be shared between components or deliberately added/omitted for balance and elegance — the first systematic license for **deliberate abbreviation** (A).[^5^] |
| 小大成形 (#30–31) | Small and large each have their proper form | Su Shi's maxim quoted in the manual: "Large characters are hard to make dense without crowding; small characters are hard to make spacious with room to spare" (大字難於結密而無間，小字難於寬綽而有餘). Hence the corollary: 大字促令小，小字放令大 — compress large characters to read smaller, open small characters to read larger, so a mixed column looks optically even (A).[^5^] |
| 相管領 (#36) | Mutual governance | Upper governs lower, the earlier leads the later; the manual explicitly extends this from the single character to the whole composition, quoting Sun Guoting's *Shupu*: "One dot sets the rule of the character; one character sets the standard for the whole piece" (一點成一字之規，一字乃終篇之準) (A).[^5^] |

Two further rules from the Ninety-Two Methods are directly relevant to matching existing lettering (A):[^6^]

- **#65–66 字形本瘦，勿使短；字形本矮，勿使瘦** — naturally narrow characters (身, 目, 耳) must not be squashed; naturally squat characters (白, 工, 日) must be drawn fuller. Character identity includes an *expected proportion class*.
- **#71–72 畫少宜肥，畫多宜勻** — characters with few strokes are written fatter; stroke-dense characters are written with uniform, restrained weight so they don't blacken into a blot.

**Designer translation (D):** these manuals amount to a rule-based layout engine for glyphs: a bounding-box policy (proportion class), a density-normalisation policy (few strokes → thicker, many → thinner), a collision policy (相讓, 避就), and a rhythm policy (排疊). When you add a character to existing lettering you are editing a running instance of this engine — you must first reverse-engineer its parameter settings.

## 3. 章法: the composition across characters

### 3.1 Columnar order and 行氣

Traditional Chinese writing runs in **vertical columns, top to bottom, sequenced right to left** (A). Within a column, what matters is 行氣 — the "breath" or energy-line of the column: the invisible centre-line along which characters hang, and the degree to which that line is allowed to sway (A/C).[^7^] In regular script (楷書) the axis is nearly straight and characters are evenly pitched; in running and cursive scripts (行書, 草書) the axis may oscillate so that each character leans into, corrects, or exaggerates the lean of the previous one — Lin Sanzhi 林散之 described the effect: "characters should be let loose, like a bird flying through trees without touching a leaf… tightness and emptiness alternate" (C, quoting his recorded talks).[^8^]

Sun Guoting's principle (一點成一字之規，一字乃終篇之準) makes the first stroke and first character of a work the tempo-setter for everything that follows (A).[^5^] A modern design analogy: the first character is the key signature.

### 3.2 Vertical vs. horizontal formats

- **Vertical formats** (立軸 hanging scroll, 條屏 panels, 對聯 couplets) are the native habitat of columnar writing; verticality lets long vertical strokes (the 懸針 "hanging needle") breathe and lets the column axis undulate (B/C).
- **Horizontal formats** are ancient too: the 手卷 (handscroll, read right-to-left in arm-length sections) and the 匾額 (horizontal plaque, historically also written right-to-left) (A). Mi Fu's *Poem Written in a Boat on the Wu River* (吳江舟中詩, ca. 1095, Met 1984.174) is a handscroll whose unusually large characters surge down the length of the paper; the Met's curatorial note records that Mi wrote it "with a suspended arm, working from the elbow rather than the wrist," entrusting the writing "to the force of the brush," even "collapsing and distorting the characters for the sake of expressiveness" (A).[^9^]
- **Modern horizontal left-to-right typesetting** breaks the native kinematics of the brush: stroke exits that were designed to drop into the next character below now point at nothing, and 行氣 has no column to live in (D). When a horizontal line of calligraphy is unavoidable, designers should expect to widen letter-spacing, re-centre each character's internal balance, and reduce linking gestures (牽絲) between characters (D).

### 3.3 計白當黑: negative space as an equal citizen

The canonical statement comes from the Qing seal-script master **Deng Shiru 鄧石如 (1743–1805)**, recorded by his pupil Bao Shichen 包世臣 in *Yizhou Shuangji* 《藝舟雙楫》: 「字畫疏處可使走馬，密處不使透風，常計白以當黑，奇趣乃出」 — "In writing, the sparse parts should admit a galloping horse; the dense parts should not admit a breath of wind; always reckon the white as if it were black — then strange delight appears" (A).[^10^] The same maxim became foundational in seal carving, where red and white masses are deliberately weighted against each other (A/C).[^11^]

A related distinction is preserved in the Qing treatise 《書筏》: 「匡廓之白，手布均齊；散亂之白，眼布均稱」 — "the whites framed inside characters are made even by the hand; the scattered whites between characters are made balanced by the eye" (A).[^7^] In other words: interior spacing is geometric; exterior spacing is **optical**. This is the historical Chinese answer to what type designers call *optical compensation*, and it is the reason mechanically even letterspacing looks wrong in calligraphy (C/D).

### 3.4 Weight distribution and movement

A composition's visual "weight" is set by ink saturation, stroke thickness and character size, and these are modulated continuously — a heavy, slow character acts as a bass note, a dry fast one as percussion (C). Yan Zhenqing's **《祭姪文稿》** (Draft of a Requiem for My Nephew, 758, National Palace Museum, Taipei) is the locus classicus: written in extreme grief after the recovery of his nephew's remains in the An Lushan war, it is a working draft — crossings-out, inserted characters, and abrupt shifts from controlled to slashing strokes are all retained, and the draft is treasured precisely *because* the composition's rhythm records emotion rather than plan (A).[^12^][^13^] Scholarly writing on the piece notes that its historical value is often eclipsed by its calligraphic fame, and that the erasures can be used to reconstruct what Yan first wrote and then deleted (C).[^13^]

## 4. Why brush mechanics produce specific shapes — and why "squiggles" fail

### 4.1 The three phases of every stroke

Every brushstroke in the tradition is analysed in three phases (A):[^14^]

1. **起筆 (entry).** The tip meets the paper either exposed (露鋒, sharp diagonal head) or concealed (藏鋒, the tip first moves *against* the stroke direction — 欲右先左, "to go right, first go left"; 欲下先上, "to go down, first go up" — then reverses, burying the point and producing a rounded head). Concealed-tip entries yield the full, inward-looking strokes prized in Yan-style regular script; exposed entries give the crisp, edged heads of Ouyang-style and much running script (A).[^14^]
2. **行筆 (movement).** The loaded brush belly is dragged while the writer modulates 提按 (lift/press). Pressing spreads the hairs and widens the trace; lifting gathers them. Whether the tip travels inside the trace (中鋒, centred tip — the ink deposits a darker spine with softer edges) or on one side (側鋒, side tip — one edge sharp, one edge ragged) is visible in the finished stroke. Speed interacts with ink load: fast dry movement yields 飛白 (flying white, bristle-split streaks) (A).[^14^]
3. **收筆 (exit).** The stroke is closed by 回鋒 (the tip reverses back into the stroke, giving a blunt "垂露" hanging-dew ending), or by lifting progressively to a tapered point (出鋒, as in 懸針 hanging-needle verticals), or is checked at a corner (頓, a pause that swells the ink into the shoulder of a hook) (A).[^14^]

Computational modelling of robotic calligraphy independently confirms that these effects are physically determined: stroke shape is governed by brush drop-height (vertical lift/press), tip lag from hair bending, and the angular deviation between brush-axis direction and travel direction — exactly the variables classical terms name (A).[^15^]

### 4.2 Turns, joins and the signature of a hand

- **轉 (rounded turn)** keeps the tip centred and rolls through the corner, giving smooth, seal-script-like bends; **折 (broken turn)** pauses (頓), re-sets the tip and changes direction, giving the angular shoulders of regular script (A).[^14^] Ouyang Xun's manner is dominated by 折; Yan Zhenqing's mature hand favours heavier 轉 (C).
- **Joins** between strokes (搭鋒) show whether the writer re-enters precisely on the previous exit or overlaps boldly — a high-frequency, low-consciousness trait that forgers and designers both miss (C/D).
- The Yuan-era distinction **內擫 / 外拓** (inner-pressing vs. outer-expanding hand mechanics, associated with Wang Xizhi vs. Wang Xianzhi) shows that even within one family style, the habitual *rotation of the hand* changes every contour in a character (C).[^16^]

### 4.3 Why arbitrary "squiggles" fail

This is the practical heart of the module. Adding free-form squiggles beside calligraphy fails, in ways that are diagnosable, not mystical (D, grounded in A):

1. **No phase structure.** A squiggle has constant curvature and width; a brushstroke has an entry swell, a governed middle, and an exit. The absence of 起/行/收 segmentation is immediately legible to any practised viewer as "not written" (B/C).
2. **No pressure rhythm.** Brush width is a function of time-varying pressure correlated with speed (slow press at turns, fast lift at exits). Squiggles drawn at constant velocity produce dead, monotonal lines — the visual equivalent of a metronome with no accent (C, corroborated by A).[^15^]
3. **No structural grammar.** A real character, however wild, obeys 結字: density normalisation, 避就 between repeated strokes, a stable centre of gravity. Even Mi Fu's celebrated distortions ride "the line between historically informed elegance and strategic imbalance" (Met) — imbalance is *strategic*, applied to a structure the writer could also write correctly (A).[^9^] Squiggles collapse structure without ever having had one.
4. **No inter-character breathing.** In running script the exit of one character aims at the entry of the next (意連 at the composition scale). Decorative squiggles point nowhere, so they break 行氣 rather than joining it (D).
5. **No abbreviation legitimacy.** Real cursive compression follows the 草法 conventions ultimately rooted in the Han 章草《急就篇》 tradition — a "simplified" character is a *correct* character in another script, not a lazier one (A).[^2^] The classical licence for 借換 and 增減 (borrowing and omitting strokes) exists *inside* this grammar (A).[^5^]

**The practical rule (D):** if you cannot state which script, whose hand, which entry/exit type, and which pressure rhythm your new stroke imitates, do not draw it. Silence (leaving space — 計白當黑) is always a legitimate compositional move; a false stroke never is.

## 5. Practical style-matching checklist for designers

Use when lettering a new character/title to accompany existing calligraphy (e.g. adding a matching 齊 to an ancestor's inscription for the Qi/Chee family book). Steps 1–9 are observation (B); the rest are craft (D).

| # | Check | What to measure or note |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Script family | 篆 / 隸 / 楷 / 行 / 草. Never mix families in one added word unless the original itself does. |
| 2 | Lineage/hand | Nearest known model (歐體, 顏體, 二王行書, 館閣體…). Copy *that master's* version of the character from a dictionary of his extant characters, not a generic font. |
| 3 | Entry type per stroke | 藏鋒 or 露鋒, and at what angle; note rounded vs. cut heads. |
| 4 | Exit type per stroke | 回鋒 (blunt), 出鋒 (tapered), or checked 頓; measure taper length relative to stroke length. |
| 5 | Pressure rhythm | Ratio of thickest to thinnest point per stroke; where the swell sits (head, middle, tail). Reproduce with a pressure-sensitive brush or multi-pass vector width profile — never a uniform stroke. |
| 6 | Turn type | 轉 (rounded) vs. 折 (angular), and corner shoulder size. |
| 7 | Proportion class | Is the character naturally tall/squat (92-fa #65–66)? Match the original's width-to-height ratio, not the typeface's. |
| 8 | Density normalisation | Few-stroke characters drawn heavier; many-stroke lighter and tighter (#71–72). Check the new character's ink area per em against neighbours. |
| 9 | Abbreviation legitimacy | If the original uses cursive forms, verify the target character's cursive form exists in that same tradition (compare 草訣 sources / the master's own writing). Do not invent shortcuts. |
| 10 | Column behaviour | Place the new character on the existing column axis; match lean (斜勢), not just size. In horizontal settings, deliberately re-balance (see §3.2). |
| 11 | Ink and ground | Match dry/wet character (潤筆 vs. 飛白), ink value, and paper tone/absorbency of the original; a wet stroke on glossy stock will never read as the same hand. |
| 12 | Spacing policy | Measure the original's 字距 (inter-character) and 行距 (inter-column) ratios — in much classical layout, especially seal/clerical, character spacing exceeds column spacing (Deng Shiru's practice: 字距大於行距) (A).[^17^] |
| 13 | Optical, not geometric, check | Step back: balance "by the eye" (書筏's 眼布均稱) — equal numeric spacing is a defect, not a goal (A).[^7^] |
| 14 | Whole-piece rehearsal | 意在筆先 — plan the entire added phrase before touching brush or pen-tool; each stroke's exit should anticipate the next entry (A).[^1^] |
| 15 | Honesty test | If a fluent calligrapher cannot read your addition as *writing*, withdraw it and use space instead (D; consequence of §4.3). |

## 7. Gaps and limitations

- The **Thirty-Six Methods** is of uncertain authorship and late (probably Song) compilation; it documents the *Ouyang manner*, not Ouyang's own teaching (C).[^3^]
- The **Ninety-Two Methods** attribution (Shao Ying vs. Huang Ziyuan) is settled only in recent Chinese scholarship and museum research; popular sources still credit Huang (C).[^4^]
- Much English-language literature on 章法 is thin; several primary texts quoted here (《書筏》, 《藝舟雙楫》) lack standard scholarly English translations — translations above are this module's own and should be reviewed before publication (limitation).
- The style-matching checklist (§5) is a **design synthesis (D)**, not a historical document; it has not been validated by working calligraphers.
- Stroke-mechanics physics (§4.1) is documented at the level of robotics research; quantitative thresholds (e.g. exact pressure–width curves per script) remain an open research topic (C/A).[^15^]
- Nothing here addresses seal-script or clerical-script composition in depth; those require a dedicated module.

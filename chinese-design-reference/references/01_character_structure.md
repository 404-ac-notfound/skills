# Module 01 — Character Structure and Variants (字形結構與異體)

**Library series:** Chinese art/design reference modules for an AI design assistant
**First application:** Chinese–English Malaysian Chinese family-history book, Qi/Chee (齊) family, Qi'an Village (齊安村), Fuzhou
**Compiled:** 2026-11-07
**Claim labels used throughout:** (A) documented fact / primary statement · (B) visual observation · (C) scholarly interpretation · (D) proposed contemporary design application

---

## 1. Why this module exists

A family-history book about the 齊 (Qi/Chee) family will inevitably put large, expressive Chinese characters on a page — the surname itself, the ancestral village name 齊安, generation-poem characters, mottoes. The moment a designer touches a character — stretches a stroke, drops a dot, merges two strokes, chooses 齐 vs 齊 — three different kinds of questions arise, and they must **not** be conflated:

1. **Linguistic facts** — what the writing system actually records: standard forms, stroke counts, radicals, pronunciations, and the documented history of each character. These are checkable against dictionaries and palaeographic sources and are not a matter of taste.
2. **Historical variants** — forms that are *attested* in the received tradition: 異體字 (variant characters), 古字/今字 pairs, clerical and seal forms, cursive abbreviations preserved in copybooks and model letters. A variant is not "anything a brush once did"; it is a form that can be pointed to in a dictionary, a stele, or a manuscript tradition.
3. **Client design preferences** — what the family wants the book to feel like: solemn, modern, archaic, personal. A client may legitimately prefer a non-standard form *as design*. That is a choice, and it should be recorded as a choice — not passed off as history.

Keeping these three columns separate is the single most important discipline this module teaches. Sections 2–6 cover the linguistic facts; section 7 the variants; section 8 the reference works; section 9 a verification workflow; section 10 the preference layer.

## 2. Traditional and simplified characters: the documented baseline

### 2.1 What "simplified" means legally and historically (A)

Character simplification in the People's Republic of China proceeded through a documented sequence of official instruments:[^3^][^4^]

| Instrument | Date | Content |
|---|---|---|
| 《第一批異體字整理表》 (First List of Standardised Variant Characters) | Published 22 Dec 1955, in force 1 Feb 1956 | 810 groups of variants; 1053 (later adjusted to 1027) variant forms retired in favour of one "correct" form each, on the principle of 從俗從簡 (follow usage, follow simplicity). |
| 《漢字簡化方案》 (Chinese Character Simplification Scheme) | Promulgated by the State Council, Jan 1956 | The core simplification scheme. |
| 《簡化字總表》 (General List of Simplified Characters) | 1964; reissued with minor adjustments Oct 1986 | 2,235 simplified characters; organised in three tables (non-radical simplifications; simplifications usable as components; characters derived by analogy). |
| 《通用規範漢字表》 (General Standard Chinese Characters List) | 2013 | The current unified PRC standard, superseding the earlier lists. |

Outside the PRC, the traditional forms remained standard: Taiwan's Ministry of Education maintains its own standard-character programme (標準字體) and the《教育部異體字字典》; Hong Kong and Macau use traditional characters with their own reference standards; **Malaysia's Chinese-medium education uses simplified characters** following the PRC model, while Malaysian Chinese temple inscriptions, genealogies, and older printed matter remain overwhelmingly traditional — a bilingual Malaysian family book therefore sits astride both standards, and which register is used for which element (title page vs. transcription of a tomb inscription) is an editorial decision to make *explicitly*. (A/C)

### 2.2 The 齊/齐 case study (A/B/C)

The family surname is itself a perfect specimen:

- **Traditional/standard historical form:** 齊 (14 strokes in the Kangxi reckoning, radical 齊 itself — radical 210 of the 214 Kangxi radicals).
- **Simplified form:** 齐, produced by drastic reduction of the traditional skeleton.
- **Historical range:** the character is attested from oracle-bone script onward; paleographic dictionaries show forms picturing three even heads of grain (麥穗之齊平), which is the standard palaeographic explanation for its meaning "even, uniform." (C)
- **Variant forms (異體):** the MOE《異體字字典》lists documented graphic variants of 齊 under its entry for the standard form;《康熙字典》records its own set of old and vulgar forms under 齊部.[^2^]

Design consequence (D): a title-page treatment in 齊 signals the genealogical/traditional register and matches pre-modern inscriptions; 齐 matches the reading experience of a PRC- or Malaysian-schooled audience. Neither is "wrong"; mixing them without a rule is.

## 3. Character anatomy: radicals, strokes, stroke order

### 3.1 Radicals (部首) (A)

The radical system was created by 許慎's《說文解字》(completed 121 CE), which organised 9,353 characters under **540 radicals (部首)**, beginning with 一 and ending with 亥.[^7^] The 214-radical system familiar today was fixed much later, in the Ming《字彙》, and adopted by the Qing《康熙字典》.[^8^] Practical consequence: **radical assignment differs between dictionaries**. 齊 is its own radical in both the Shuowen and Kangxi systems; but many characters sit under different radicals in 說文, 康熙, and modern PRC dictionaries (which use a 201-radical table). When citing a radical, name the system. (A)

### 3.2 Stroke types (筆畫) (A)

Modern analysis recognises five basic stroke types — 橫 (horizontal), 豎 (vertical), 撇 (left-falling), 捺/點 (right-falling/dot), 折 (turn) — with 提 (rising) and 鉤 (hook) variously counted. Calligraphy tradition, however, codified the same material through the **永字八法** (Eight Principles of Yong), in which the single character 永 is decomposed into eight named brush gestures:[^5^][^6^]

| 八法 name | Stroke | Traditional imagery | Brush logic (C) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 側 cè | dot 點 | "a stone dropping from a peak" | side-brush entry, weight, then collection |
| 勒 lē | horizontal 橫 | "reining in a horse" | restrained, slightly arched travel, not flat |
| 弩 nǔ | vertical 豎 | "a drawn crossbow" | straight but tensile, never rigid |
| 趯 tì | hook 鉤 | "a leaping hare" | press, gather, snap out |
| 策 cè | rising 提 | "flicking a whip" | lightening pressure upward-right |
| 掠 lüè | long left-fall 撇 | "a comb passing through hair" | gradual release of pressure over length |
| 啄 zhuó | short left-fall | "a bird pecking" | short, sharp, decisive |
| 磔 zhé | right-falling 捺 | "cleaving" | wave-like pressure build ("one wave, three bends") |

Note the layering, and note what the common diagram (Visual exemplar CS-03) makes explicit: 永 as written today has **five strokes** but embodies **eight principles** — several principles ride inside a single unbroken stroke. (A/B) This is exactly the ambiguity that matters when judging cursive forms: the writing system counts *strokes* (where the brush lifts), while calligraphic analysis counts *gestures* (pressure events). A character can lose visible strokes in cursive without losing gestures, and vice versa. (C)

### 3.3 Stroke order (筆順) (A)

Stroke order is a real, codified norm (PRC:《現代漢語通用字筆順規範》, 1997; Taiwan and Japan have their own, sometimes differing, norms). It matters for designers for one main reason: **cursive and running-script abbreviations are only legible against the expected stroke order** — a 草書 form is a *re-tracing* of the regular-script writing path at speed, not a free squiggle. (C) But see Module 02, §6: stroke order cannot be *reliably reconstructed* from a finished static work alone.

## 4. Historical variants (異體字) — categories and status

### 4.1 Terminology (A)

- **異體字 (variant characters):** characters identical in sound and meaning but different in graphic form (e.g., 够/夠, 床/牀).《說文解字》called them 重文. Scholarship distinguishes 完全異體字 (fully interchangeable) from 部分異體字 (interchangeable only in some words).[^9^]
- **Which form counts as the "variant" is jurisdiction-dependent.** The 1955 PRC list chose one standard per group; Taiwan's standard sometimes chose the *other* member — e.g., in the PRC 够 is standard and 夠 the retired variant, while Taiwan treats 夠 as standard.[^9^] For a cross-strait/diaspora book this is a genuine editorial minefield. (A)
- **古今字 (ancient–modern character pairs):** the older graph and the later graph created to split off one of its senses (莫/暮, 然/燃). These are *not* freely interchangeable variants: they belong to different textual periods. (A)
- **通假字 (phonetic loans):** a contemporaneous substitution of a same/near-sound character for another (蚤 for 早). A loan character in a quoted classical text is *textual evidence*, not a "design option." If you quote an ancestor's letter or a genealogy colophon, reproduce its characters as written and annotate — do not silently "correct" them. (A/C)[^10^]

### 4.2 Calligraphic abbreviation vs. character variant — the critical distinction (C)

A 異體字 is a **graphic variant of the same script level** (two ways to write the character in regular script). A 草書 form is something else: a **systematic, tradition-bound abbreviation** of the regular form. The tradition is documented in two great reference frames:

1. **《草訣百韻歌》** (Caojue Baiyun Ge) — a rhymed mnemonic, in the received Ming copy by 韓道亨, pairing look-alike cursive forms with their distinguishing features ("有點方為水，空挑即是言" — "with a dot it's the water radical; an empty rising stroke is the speech radical"). It is the traditional primer for *reading* cursive.[^11^]
2. **于右任《標準草書》** (Yu Youren's Standard Cursive, 1930s onward) — a 20th-century codification that analysed historical cursive into a small set of **代表符號** (representative symbols): e.g., 27 left-side symbols, 23 right-side symbols, 7 top symbols, 7 bottom symbols, each standing in for a whole family of radicals (the 心 symbol also covers 忄 and 爿; the 月 symbol covers 月, 舟, 肉). Yu's stated principles were 易識、易寫、準確、美麗 (easy to read, easy to write, accurate, beautiful).[^11^]

The upshot for verification: **a cursive form of a character is legitimate if and only if it is attested** — in a recognised model work (法帖), a copybook, a dictionary of cursive forms (《草書大字典》 type resources, now searchable online via 國學大師 or 漢典書法), or derivable from a documented abbreviation rule. "It looks fast and loose" is not attestation. (A/C)

### 4.3 Distinguishing documented cursive from accidental omission or invented glyph (C, with workflow in §9)

| Signal | Documented cursive form | Accidental omission / invented glyph |
|---|---|---|
| Findable in references | Appears in 草訣百韻歌-type lists, 標準草書, a 法帖, or a cursive dictionary | Found nowhere |
| Structural logic | Follows a known abbreviation rule: component → symbol (e.g., 訁→ 言字旁 symbol), stroke fusion along writing path | Breaks the character's radical structure arbitrarily; drops a semantically load-bearing component |
| Consistency | The same writer writes the same character the same way; matches other masters' forms | One-off; inconsistent within the same document |
| Legibility to tradition | A trained reader of cursive can identify it | Even specialists cannot read it |
| Context | Found in running/cursive works where abbreviation is genre-appropriate | Found in contexts (inscriptions, seals, titles) where abbreviation is out of genre |

Two cautions. First, **attested does not mean universally accepted**: Yu Youren's own notes repeatedly flag pairs that historical cursive confuses (e.g., 郎/郅) and prescribe disambiguating workarounds — the tradition itself polices ambiguity.[^11^] Second, **genre matters**: a cursive abbreviation that is perfectly attested is still a *cursive* form. Setting a tomb inscription or a title page in cursive-derived glyph shapes imports an informality that may be culturally wrong for the object. (C/D)

## 6. A practical verification workflow for designers

When a character form in the book — a scanned ancestor's signature, a rubbing, a proposed logo glyph — is in doubt, run this sequence. Each step cites the kind of evidence produced.

1. **Fix the target identity.** Which *character* is this supposed to be, in which *standard* (PRC simplified / Taiwan standard / Hong Kong / historical)? Write it down in both 齊 and 齐 form where relevant. (A)
2. **Look it up in 漢典.** Record: standard forms, stroke count, radical (Kangxi), the 字源字形 evolution strip, and the listed 異體. Two minutes.[^12^]
3. **Cross-check the variant in the MOE《異體字字典》.** If the form is claimed as a variant, it should appear here with citations. If it appears, you have an (A)-grade attestation of the *relationship*; note which side Taiwan calls standard.[^2^]
4. **Cross-check in 國學大師** for the《漢語大字典》and《康熙字典》page images — screenshot the actual page. If a "variant" appears in no dictionary, downgrade it from "variant" to "unattested form."[^14^]
5. **If the form is cursive/running:** check (a) a cursive dictionary (草書大字典 via 國學大師; 漢典書法), (b) whether it matches a《標準草書》symbol rule, (c) whether it matches the form in a named model work. Require at least one hit.[^11^][^14^]
6. **If the form is old (seal/clerical/epigraphic):** check《說文》small-seal form (szsw.bnu.edu.cn) and, for pre-Qin claims, Academia Sinica's excavated-text databases. Do not let a *stylised modern seal-font* pose as 小篆 — compare against the dictionary glyph.[^13^]
7. **Classify and label the outcome in the project file:** (i) standard form, (ii) documented variant (cite dictionary + page), (iii) documented cursive abbreviation (cite copybook/model), (iv) unattested — treat as design invention or error. Only after classification does the aesthetic conversation begin. (D)
8. **Record client preference separately.** If the client prefers an unattested form, document it as "design adaptation, not a historical form," and keep it out of contexts where it would be read as documentary (quoted genealogies, inscriptions). (D)

**Rule of thumb (C):** absence of evidence is not proof of error — minor hands and regional habits produced many forms — but in a published family history the burden of proof sits on the unusual form: no attestation, no claim.

## 8. Gaps and limitations

- **Standard conflicts are unresolved by design.** PRC, Taiwan, Hong Kong, and Malaysia/Singapore standards differ on specific glyphs (e.g., 夠/够; 裏/裡; bone/meat radical forms). This module does not adjudicate; the book's style sheet must. (A)
- **Online aggregators drift.** 漢典 and 國學大師 transcriptions contain occasional errors; for anything published, cite the page image. (A)
- **Cursive attestation is probabilistic.** Even《標準草書》is a 20th-century codification with critics; "documented cursive" should ideally mean two independent attestations (e.g., a Tang model work + a cursive dictionary). (C)
- **Malaysian-specific orthographic practice** (e.g., traditional-character conventions in Malaysian temple and genealogical carving, simplified in schools) is asserted here from general knowledge of the Malaysian Chinese education context and should be verified against Malaysian sources (e.g., 董總 textbooks, local epigraphy surveys) before publication claims are made. **(unverified)**
- **The palaeographic account of 齊** (grain-ear pictograph) is the standard scholarly reading (C); designers should not flatten it into "the character means wheat."
- Some Commons file pages listed in the register were verified via search-index records and embedded thumbnails rather than full page-loads; image licensing is as stated on those records (noted per row).

# Module 07 — Fuzhou / Fujian and Malaysian Chinese Context

**Scope.** Contextual reference for a Chinese–English Malaysian Chinese family-history book (example working context: a family surnamed 齊, with roots described as "Qi'an Village, Fuzhou"). This module documents *regional* practice — Fuzhou (福州) and the Fuzhou-descended communities of Malaysia — as distinct from generic "Chinese culture."

**Evidence labels used inline:** (A) documented fact / primary statement; (B) visual observation; (C) scholarly interpretation; (D) proposed contemporary design application.

**Hard rule for this whole module:** naming conventions, dialect romanisations, and clan practices are *context*, not *evidence*. They must never be used to infer any actual person's identity, relationship, birthplace, or ancestry. See §6.

---

## 1. Fuzhou and Eastern Fujian: the sending region

Fuzhou (福州) is the capital of Fujian Province and the cultural centre of the Mindong (閩東, "Eastern Min") region. The counties that supplied most Fuzhou-group emigrants include Minhou (閩侯), Minqing (閩清), Gutian (古田), Fuqing (福清), Changle (長樂), Lianjiang (連江), Yongtai (永泰), Pingnan (屏南), and Pingtan (平潭) (A).[^1^]

A critical regional distinction: Fujian is *not* linguistically or culturally uniform. The Fuzhou dialect belongs to the **Eastern Min** branch and is **mutually unintelligible** with Hokkien (Southern Min, 閩南話), the variety spoken by the largest Chinese dialect group in Malaysia and Singapore (A).[^1^] A design or editorial pipeline that treats "Fujian = Hokkien" will make factual errors: Hokkien temple vocabulary, Hokkien romanisations, and Hokkien kinship terms do not describe Fuzhou communities (C).

## 2. Fuzhou migration to Southeast Asia and the Sibu "New Fuzhou" settlement

### 2.1 The documented Sibu settlement (the best-documented Fuzhou migration case)

The settlement of Sibu (詩巫), Sarawak, on the Rajang (拉讓) River, is the canonical case of organised Fuzhou migration to Southeast Asia, and it is unusually well documented (A):

- In **1900**, Wong Nai Siong (黃乃裳, 1849–1924), a Methodist-affiliated reformer from Minqing County, scouted the Rajang River basin and signed a cultivation agreement with Charles Brooke, the second White Rajah of Sarawak, to establish an agricultural colony named **"New Fuzhou" (新福州墾場)** (A).[^2^][^3^]
- Migrants arrived in **three organised batches**: a first party of 91 departed Fuzhou on 23 December 1900, of whom **72 arrived** at Sungai Merah (新珠山) on 20 February 1901; a second party of **535** led by Wong himself departed 7 February 1901 and landed in March 1901 (Gutian migrants at Wong Nai Siong's associate's site 黃師來/王士来, Minqing migrants at Sibu); a third party of about **511–535** recruited in early 1902 arrived on **7 June 1902** — roughly **1,118 settlers in total**, of whom about two-thirds were Christians (A).[^2^][^4^]
- Wong renamed the Sungai Merah ("Red River", for its reddish water) settlement area **新厝安 (Seduan)**, expressing the hope that settlers could "open the land and settle their homes" (A).[^2^]
- Wong left Sibu in 1904 and never returned; settlement leadership passed to the American Methodist missionary **James M. Hoover**, who managed the community from 1904 to 1935 and is credited locally with building dozens of churches and schools (A).[^3^][^5^]
- Sibu retains the nicknames "New Fuzhou" (新福州) and "Little Fuzhou" (小福州), and Fuzhou-descended institutions — guilds, churches, schools, a Wong Nai Siong Memorial Garden and a James Hoover Memorial Park along the Sungai Merah Heritage Walk — remain part of the town's public landscape (A).[^5^][^6^]

Scholars emphasise that this was a distinctive migration pattern: unlike the largely spontaneous chain migration of Hokkien communities, the Sibu settlement was an *organised, recruiter-led, contract-based* colonisation project with a strong Methodist Episcopal Church network behind it (C).[^4^][^7^]

**Design relevance (D).** For a family-history book about a Fuzhou-descended Malaysian family, the Sibu material offers documented dates, place names in three writing systems (新珠山 / Sungai Merah; 新厝安 / Seduan; 詩巫 / Sibu), and a migration narrative arc (recruitment → three sailings → settlement → institutions) that can structure chapter design, timeline graphics, and map plates. Do not, however, assume any given Malaysian Fuzhou-descended family came via Sibu — Fuzhou migrants also settled elsewhere in Sarawak (Kuching, Miri, Bintulu), in Sitiawan/實兆遠 and Yong Peng (永平) in Peninsular Malaysia, and in Sabah (C; the association lists in §7 document this geography).[^8^]

### 2.2 Fuzhou dialect (福州話) and its romanisation

- The Fuzhou dialect calls itself **福州話 (Hók-ciŭ-uâ)** or **平話 (Bàng-uâ, "the everyday speech")** (A).[^1^]
- **Foochow Romanized** (福州話羅馬字, also **Bàng-uâ-cê 平話字, BUC**) is a Latin orthography devised by American and English missionaries in the mid-19th century, standardised over following decades, and used chiefly in church circles and mission schools; a Bible in Foochow Romanized was published by the British and Foreign Bible Society in 1908 (A).[^1^]
- Wong Nai Siong himself compiled a two-volume 《福州音普通字教科書》 combining the traditional rime book 《戚林八音》's initials and finals with Latin letters to spell Fuzhou speech (A).[^2^]
- English-language spellings of the dialect name vary: Foochow, Hokchew, Hokchiu, Hok-chiu, Fuzhounese (A).[^1^] Malaysian English usage frequently prefers **"Foochow"** or **"Hokchiu"**, and Malaysian association names use **"Foochow"** (e.g., Persatuan Foochow Selangor & Kuala Lumpur = 雪隆福州會館) (A).[^9^]

**Design relevance (D).** If the book includes Fuzhou-dialect words (terms of address, place names, food), BUC is the historically authentic romanisation, but it uses diacritics (ŏ, ĕ̤, ṳ̄, à̤) that demand fonts with full Latin Extended + combining-mark support (see Module 08 on fonts). A practical convention is to give Chinese characters + BUC + English gloss on first occurrence, and to record the family's *own remembered spellings* verbatim in captions rather than silently re-romanising them (D).

## 3. Ancestral halls (祠堂) and genealogy (族譜/家譜) practice in Fujian

### 3.1 Ancestral halls

In Fujian, the ancestral hall (祠堂, also 宗祠, 祖祠, 家廟) is the ritual and administrative centre of a lineage: it houses spirit tablets (神主), hosts ancestral rites, and historically served as the venue for clan governance — deliberating clan affairs, managing clan property, enforcing clan rules, compiling the genealogy, and conducting capping, wedding and funeral rites (A).[^10^] Each hall carries a hall name — a 郡號 (commandery name) or 堂號 (hall name) — by which descendants identify their lineage's claimed place of origin; Fujian examples include 潁川堂 (Chen), 江夏堂 (Huang), 六桂堂, and 太原堂 (Wang) (A).[^10^] The popularisation of lineage halls is conventionally traced to a 1536 memorial by Xia Yan permitting commoners to erect family temples and sacrifice to first ancestors (A).[^10^]

Note that "hall name" (堂號) is a *lineage* identifier, not a family-address identifier, and the same surname can carry several different hall names; conversely a hall name alone does not prove village-level origin (C).

### 3.2 Genealogies (族譜/家譜/宗譜)

Chinese genealogies follow a recognisable repertoire of components. A standard enumeration includes: prefaces (譜序); editorial principles (凡例/譜例); lists of compilers (修譜名目); imperial patents and eulogies (誥敕, 像贊); clan rules and instructions (宗規家訓); the **genealogical charts proper (世系表/世系圖)**; biographical records (世系錄/齒錄); **generation-poem character cycles (派語/班派/字輩)**; hall and grave diagrams (祠堂圖, 墓圖); and records of clan property (祠產) (A).[^11^]

Several conventions matter directly for book design:

- **Five-generation chart units (五世一提).** Surviving genealogies state that charts run "from high ancestor down to great-great-grandson, divided into generations" and restart every five generations — the so-called 歐蘇式 (Ouyang Xiu / Su Xun) model: "世系圖遵歐蘇合式以五世為提取五服之義也" (A).[^12^] This modular five-generation block is the traditional solution to exactly the layout problem a modern designer faces with wide descent charts (see Module 08, §5) (D).
- **Recorded fields per person.** Editorial rules (凡例) specify that under each person's taboo name (諱) the genealogy records 字 (courtesy name), 行 (birth order), 號 (art name), offices and degrees, birth and death dates, spouse(s), and burial site — "諱下書字書行書號書職官及科甲生監詳載生卒" — and that grave and tablet inscriptions be recorded so that sacrifices are not forgotten (A).[^12^]
- **Generation names (字輩/派語).** Lineages maintain a generation poem (字輩詩), typically in four-, five-, or seven-character lines; members of the same generation share one character of the poem in their given name (usually the middle character). The purposes recorded are to order generations and distinguish seniority (尊世系、辨昭穆), to distinguish branches and degree of kinship, and to avoid duplicate names and naming taboos (A).[^13^][^14^] Malaysian Chinese families of all dialect groups commonly continue generation-name practice into the 20th century, though usage weakened in later generations (C).
- **Adoption and succession rules.** Genealogies record adopted heirs explicitly, requiring that the adoption be noted under both the natal and adoptive father ("出繼某人"), reflecting how seriously the format treats unambiguous lineage accounting (A).[^15^]

**Design relevance (D).** A family-history book can legitimately borrow genealogy apparatus — a 凡例-style "editorial notes" page, generation-poem display, five-generation chart modules — as long as the book does not claim to *be* a clan genealogy. Bilingual presentation should keep the vertical-text, right-to-left feel of traditional chart pages for heritage plates while using horizontal bilingual text for narrative pages (D).

## 4. Inscription conventions: gravestones (墓碑) and spirit tablets (神主)

### 4.1 Gravestone format

Malaysian Chinese cemetery practice is documented in the handbook published by the Kuala Lumpur & Selangor Chinese cemetery association (馬來西亞華人義山殯葬文化手冊, published by 吉隆坡廣東義山, ktc.org.my) (A):[^16^]

- The **central column (中榜)** carries the deceased's name with honorific kin terms: a father is styled 考 or 顯考, a mother 妣 or 顯妣; men receive 公/府君, women 氏/孺人. Example formats given include 「顯考某某府君之靈」「顯妣某氏老孺人之靈」(A).[^16^][^17^]
- **Birth and death dates** flank the central column, conventionally 男左女右 (male left, female right) (A).[^16^]
- **Native place (籍貫/地望).** In overseas Chinese cemeteries the stone's upper corners record the deceased's ancestral origin — province and county, sometimes village — because the dead were considered 客死異鄉 ("dead away from home") and buried in shared public cemeteries (義山/公塚); studies of Indonesian and Malaysian stones show formats ranging from prefecture names (e.g., 漳州) down to village names (A).[^18^][^16^] The Malaysian handbook lists Fuzhou-region origins explicitly: 閩侯、古田、長樂、屏南、連江、閩清、福清 etc. (A).[^16^]
- **Script and carving conventions.** The handbook advises against running or cursive script on stones, recommending dignified 魏碑 (Wei stele), 隸書 (clerical) or 楷書 (regular) script, and specifies intaglio (陰刻) rather than relief carving (A).[^16^]
- Character-count superstitions (生、老、病、死、苦 cycles, "riding the yellow path" 過黃道) exist but the handbook itself notes they need not be rigidly followed (A).[^16^]

### 4.2 Spirit tablets (神主)

Spirit tablets in the ancestral hall follow the same honorific grammar as gravestones (顯考/顯妣 + surname + 府君/孺人), and genealogical editorial rules require that hall, grave and tablet texts be consistent so that descendants can perform rites correctly (A).[^12^]

**Design relevance (D).** These conventions give a ready-made typographic hierarchy for memorial plates in the book: honorific + name as the display line, dates flanking, native place as a marginal notation. When reproducing photographs of actual family graves or tablets, reproduce them as documents (transcribe + translate), and never "correct" the stone's wording or spellings (D).

## 5. Photographic-album practices

This area is the thinnest in documented scholarship located for this module. What can be stated: (A) Chinese and overseas-Chinese families of the 20th century commonly kept studio-portrait and family albums, and commemorative albums (紀念冊) were and are routinely published by Malaysian clan associations and schools for anniversaries; (C) researchers of Chinese family photography note the album's role in maintaining kinship across migration distance, but family-album conventions (captioning, ancestor-first ordering, inclusion of grave and hometown photos) vary by family and should be documented from the family's own albums rather than assumed. ## 6. Malaysian Chinese naming: characters, dialect spellings, and pinyin in coexistence

### 6.1 How one person ends up with several spellings

Malaysian birth registration historically recorded Chinese names in romanised form only, and the spelling was usually based on the **family's dialect pronunciation as heard and written down by a registrar**, not on any standard system (C).[^19^] Consequently:

- The same character yields different registered spellings by dialect: e.g., 陳 appears as Tan (Hokkien), Chan (Cantonese), Chin (Hakka), Tang (Teochew), Ting/Teng (other Min varieties) (A).[^20^]
- The same *spelling* maps to different characters: "Chia" is documented for 謝 (Hokkien/Teochew/Hakka); "Chee" is documented for 徐 (Hokkien/Teochew/Gan) (A).[^21^]
- Within one family, siblings' documents may spell the surname differently (registrar variance, re-registration), and later generations may adopt Hanyu Pinyin spellings, so a single person can legitimately appear as, e.g., characters 齊 + registered "Chee" + pinyin "Qi" across documents (C).

### 6.2 The surname 齊 across systems

Documented readings of 齊 (A, from Wiktionary's cited dictionaries):[^22^]

| System | Reading of 齊 |
|---|---|
| Hanyu Pinyin (Mandarin) | Qí |
| Wade–Giles | Chʻi² |
| Gwoyeu Romatzyh | Chyi |
| Cantonese (Jyutping) | cai4 |
| Hokkien (POJ) | chôe (vernacular) / chê (literary) |
| Teochew (Peng'im) | ci5 / zoi5 |
| **Fuzhou / Hokchiu (BUC)** | **cà̤** |
| Hakka (Sixian, Pha̍k-fa-sṳ) | chhè / chhì |

A Republican-era bilingual surname table (《華英中國姓氏表》) lists 齊 as Gwoyeu Romatzyh "Chyi" / Wade "Ch'i" (B, scan on Wikimedia Commons).[^23^]

**Caution (important).** The spelling "Chee" is documented in reference works as a Hokkien/Teochew romanisation of **徐**, not of 齊; the documented Fuzhou (BUC) reading of 齊 is *cà̤*. Therefore, in a Malaysian context, a registered "Chee" spelling and the character 齊 may coexist for one family through non-standard or transcription-based romanisation, but the library must record this as an open question about *that family's documents* — not resolve it by rule. Reverse inference (spelling → character, or spelling → dialect group → ancestry) is unreliable and is explicitly out of scope (C).[^21^][^22^]

### 6.3 Editorial rules for the design assistant (D)

1. Preserve every attested spelling exactly as it appears on each document, with the document type and date.
2. Treat the Chinese characters as the canonical identifier; treat romanisations as historical records of pronunciation, not as errors.
3. On first use of a name, present characters + registered spelling (+ pinyin in brackets if useful); afterwards use the family's own preferred spelling.
4. Never merge or split person records on the basis of spelling similarity alone.

## 7. Malaysian Chinese institutions relevant to Fuzhou-descended families

The clan-association (會館/公會) landscape is documented through association directories (A):[^8^][^9^]

- **世界福州十邑同鄉總會** (World Federation of Fuzhou Associations) lists Malaysian member bodies including: 馬來西亞福州社團聯會總會 (Federation of Foochow Associations of Malaysia), 詩巫福州公會, 砂拉越福州社團聯合總會, 古晉/美里/民都魯/泗里街/林夢/成邦江/馬拉端福州公會, 詩巫閩清同鄉會, 曼絨(實兆遠)福州會館/閩清同鄉會/三山同鄉會, 斗湖福州公會, 沙巴亞庇福州公會, 納閩福州公會, and **雪隆福州會館 (Persatuan Foochow Selangor & Kuala Lumpur)** (A).[^8^]
- "十邑" refers to the ten traditional counties of the Fuzhou region — the identity frame is **county-region based**, not surname based (A).[^8^]
- Surname-based clan associations (宗親會, e.g. 李氏總會, 許氏總會) and cemetery-management bodies (義山, e.g. 吉隆坡廣東義山, which co-publishes the national Qingming memorial) form parallel structures; a family may belong to both a locality association and a surname association (A).[^9^][^16^]

**Design relevance (D).** Association publications (anniversary 紀念特刊), association halls, and 義山 records are the practical archives for a Malaysian family-history project; the book's credits/acknowledgements pages should follow the local convention of naming associations in both Chinese and registered Malay/English names (D).

## 9. Gaps and limitations

1. **"Qi'an Village, Fuzhou" is unverified.** No gazetteer match for a Fuzhou-region village of that exact name was located in this research pass; possible characters (齊安/旗安/岐安…) and county affiliation must be established from family documents, not guessed. This is the family's private fact to supply.
2. **Malaysian registration practice** is described here from secondary/scholarly sources; no National Registration Department (JPN) primary document on historical Chinese-name registration procedures was obtained.
3. **Family photographic-album practice** lacks a citable specialist study in this pass (§5).
4. **Foochow Romanized typography** specifics (exact diacritic stack needed for BUC) should be confirmed against a BUC reference grammar before typesetting dialect text.
5. Tombstone and tablet conventions vary by cemetery, dialect group, and era; §4 documents one Malaysian handbook and should not be universalised.
6. Nothing in this module may be used to infer the identity, relationships, or ancestry of any private individual.

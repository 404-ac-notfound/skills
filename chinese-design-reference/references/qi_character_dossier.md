## 2. Paleography and structure

### 2.1 Traditional explanation (說文解字 tradition)

《說文解字 · 卷七 · 齊部》: 「齊，禾麥吐穗上平也。象形。凡齊之屬皆从齊。徂兮切。」 — "Qi: the evenness at the top of grain and wheat as they put forth ears. Pictograph." (A) 齊 heads its own Shuowen section (齊部), which carried over as Kangxi radical 210. (A)

Xu Kai's (徐鍇) commentary appended in Shuowen: 「生而齊者莫若禾麥。二，地也。兩傍在低處也。」 — "Nothing growing is so even as grain and wheat. The 二 is the ground; the two side strokes sit at the lower level." (A) Duan Yucai's 說文解字注 elaborates: 「从二者、象地有高下也。禾麥隨地之高下爲高下。似不齊而實齊。」 — the ground line 二 marks uneven terrain; the grain tops adjust to it, "seemingly uneven yet truly even" — an important nuance: the etymon encodes *level tops despite uneven ground*, not mechanical uniformity. (A)

### 2.2 Character formation by stage

- **甲骨文 (oracle bone, Shang):** the graph depicts three grain stalks whose seed-heads crest at the same height; dictionary sources describe it as 「象禾麥穗頭長得平整的樣子」. (A) — see exemplar QI-01 (Commons, ACC project, source ID HJ00098).
- **金文 (bronze, Western Zhou and later):** the ground line 二 is added beneath the three heads — 「金文下邊又畫出了地面」. In bronze inscriptions the graph is used predominantly as the state name 齊 (Qi). (A) — see QI-02 (Commons, source ID JC04216).
- **小篆 (small seal, as fixed in Shuowen):** regularised symmetrical form; the Commons ACC file notes it "can be drawn with 17 pen strokes" in seal technique, with the lower 二 flanked by the two falling side strokes. (A/B) — see QI-03.
- **隸變 → 楷書:** clerical "silkworm-head" horizontals flatten the silhouette; the standard 楷書 form has the anatomy: top 亠 (dot + horizontal); a middle register of paired diagonal/grain-head strokes (commonly analysed as 刀 + 丫 + a second 刀-like element, ⿲刀丫𱍸); and a base of **two horizontal levels 二 flanked by a left leg 丿 and a right leg 丨/亅**. (A)

### 2.3 Alternative scholarly interpretation

Shirakawa Shizuka (白川靜, 《說文新義》 / 《字通》) rejects the grain reading and proposes the graph depicts a woman's hairpin arrangement (婦人簪飾之象), connecting the character to the "reverent" (齋/齊敬) sense used in ritual contexts. (C) This is a minority dissent against the mainstream 說文 tradition; both should be cited in any design narrative, with 說文 as the orthodox account and Shirakawa flagged as an alternative.

**Simplification logic 齊 → 齐:** the simplified form is a grass-script-derived vulgar abbreviation (草書楷化-type simplification, (C)) that was already in circulation as a 俗字 by the Song–Yuan period (取經詩話 citation above, (A)); the PRC simplification scheme adopted it in 1956/1964. The simplified 齐 keeps the top 亠-like element and the two falling strokes plus the two legs, discarding the entire middle register of grain-heads.

## 4. Structural analysis: running and cursive 齊 vs. standard 楷書

Baseline 楷書 anatomy (A): crown 亠 → middle grain-head cluster (paired diagonals, "乂"-like crossings) → base 二 with legs 丿 (left, down-sweeping) and 丨/亅 (right, descending, often with hook). Analyses below are (C) interpretations based on the documented sources; the specific glyph images should be re-checked against the rubbings before typographic work (see Gaps).

| Example | Script | Preserved | Compressed / joined | Implied / dropped | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 顏勤禮碑 (顏真卿, 779) | 楷書 (baseline variant) | Full 14-stroke anatomy; broad horizontals, weighty verticals | Middle diagonals tightened toward the central axis (Yan-style 內擫 density) | — | Reference standard for "monumental" 齊; the two base horizontals 二 are emphatic and level (B, to confirm on rubbing) |
| 蜀素帖 (米芾, 1088, 「龜鶴年壽齊」) | 行書 | Crown 亠 and the two legs remain legible; right leg 丨 anchors the character | Middle cluster runs as connected diagonal strokes; base 二 often merges into a single rising-falling movement | Separations between middle strokes are implied by brush rhythm rather than gaps | Mi Fu's hand is documented as "偏縱長傾側" — expect an elongated, right-leaning silhouette (A for style, B to confirm) |
| 爭座位帖 (顏真卿, 764, 「以齊桓公之盛業」) | 行書 (draft/稿書) | Character remains fully readable; base legs present | Crown and middle partially ligatured; strokes joined in one breath per the "字相連屬飛動" character of the work (A: contemporary criticism by 米芾) | Second horizontal of 二 may be reduced to a flick | Draft manuscript aesthetic: corrections and 圈去 marks elsewhere in the piece show the working-hand nature |
| 草訣百韻歌 (韓道亨, 1613) | 草書 | A recognisable crown + descending spine | Middle cluster collapsed into 2–3 shorthand strokes; base 二 + legs merged into a closing curve/hook | Most internal detail implied | The rhyme 「齋齊曾不較」 documents that standard cursive does **not** distinguish 齋 from 齊 (A); 「欲識高齊馬」 documents that cursive 齊 is visually close to cursive 高 and 馬, distinguished only by small differentiators (A) |
| 于右任 標準草書 (1936, if confirmed) | 草書 (standardised) | Crown + two-stroke base | Same compression as historical cursive, regularised under the 易識/易寫/準確/美麗 principles (A) | — | **unverified** occurrence; if located, it would give the modern "canonical" cursive skeleton |

**Design-relevant summary (C):** across documented running/cursive forms, what survives of 齊 is (i) the dotted crown 亠, (ii) a fast middle "zipper" of diagonals, and (iii) the two-legged base read as a vertical–hook pair. What vanishes first is the internal grain-head symmetry — precisely the feature that carries the etymology.

## 5. CLIENT DESIGN PREFERENCE — design assessment, NOT historical claim

> **(D) This entire section is a design-preference assessment.** Nothing here is a claim about what historical forms of 齊 "should" look like, and no ancient precedent is asserted for the client's preferred rendering.

The client's existing preferred design (as described; **no artwork was supplied**) features:
1. **two lower horizontal levels**,
2. a **left leg that finishes upward** (upward-flicking 丿/hook),
3. a **right leg that finishes downward** (descending 丨).

Assessment against documented material:

- **Two lower horizontal levels (二):** this matches the standard 楷書 anatomy of 齊 directly — the base 二 is structural, present from the bronze forms (the "ground line") through 隸書 and 楷書 (A). A design retaining two distinct lower levels is *compatible with* the orthodox structure.
- **Right leg finishing downward:** matches the standard form (the right 丨/亅 descends below the base line in 楷書) (A/B).
- **Left leg finishing upward:** in standard 楷書 the left leg 丿 sweeps *down-left*; an **upward finish** is a ligature gesture that occurs in 行書/草書 practice when the brush exits toward the next stroke or closes the base with a returning hook (C). It is a legitimate calligraphic behaviour but is *not* the dictionary-standard termination. No attempt has been made to find an ancient stele that "proves" this feature, and none should be cited.
- Because no client artwork was provided, **a specific style-matching assessment is not possible yet**. Needed: a scan/photo of the preferred rendering (stroke order, terminal directions, weights), so each feature can be matched individually against the documented exemplars in Section 3 and the register.

**Malaysian/Singapore spelling — important cautions:**
- Malaysia has **no official romanisation standard** for Chinese names; spellings follow the family's dialect and the registering clerk (A — Wikipedia "Malaysian names").
- **Cheah almost always = 謝** (Hokkien Siā), *not* 齊. Do not conflate. (A, well-documented Malaysian pattern)
- **Chee** is plausible for 齊 (it sits naturally between Wade–Giles Ch'i and several dialect outputs, and "chee" is the standard English respelling of pinyin qi, e.g. in learner materials), but it is **also used for other surnames** (e.g. 徐, 朱, 施 in various dialects). No source located this session documents a *Malaysian* Chee family as 齊 specifically — **unverified for this family**; the family's own registration documents are the authority.
- For a Foochow (Fuzhou) family, dialect-consistent spellings of 齊 would be expected to look like *Cheh/Che/Chea*; no documented Malaysian example was found — **unverified**. The Foochow precedent on record in Malaysia is for 陳 → Ding/Ting (A), which shows how strongly Foochow spellings diverge from Mandarin.
- Indonesian-era spellings to watch in older documents: Tjhe/Tjee-type forms (Dutch orthography), though none verified for 齊 this session.

**Public cultural reference point:** 齊白石 **Qi Baishi** (1864–1957), painter and seal-carver, is the world's best-known bearer of this surname (A). His own seals frequently featured his surname — documented examples include the self-used seals 「齊大」, 「齊璜」, 「齊人」, 「老齊」 and the gift seal 「見賢思齊」 (A; catalogue listings in 齊白石全集 vol. 8). His single-stroke, one-side-smooth/one-side-rough (單刀直衝) seal-cutting manner is a major 20th-century reference for how 齊 can be structured in seal script design (C). Museum-grade images of these seals are held by the Beijing Fine Art Academy (北京畫院); openly licensed impressions were **not** located this session (see Gaps).
